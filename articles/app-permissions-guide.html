App Permissions Explained: What to Allow and What to Deny

By TechSaathi Team · Updated: August 29, 2026 · ~6 min read

Install a new app and the questions start flying: "Allow access to your contacts? Your location? Your microphone?" Most of us tap Allow to make the popup go away — and that's exactly how apps end up knowing everything about you. Permissions are not just popups; they're doors into your personal life. Here's how to decide which doors to open.

The Permissions That Actually Matter

There are dozens of permission types, but these few deserve most of your attention:

PermissionReasonable ForRed Flag In
LocationMaps, cab apps, weatherGames, torch, calculator
MicrophoneVoice notes, calls, recorderPhoto editors, simple games
CameraCamera apps, scanning, video callsNote apps, file managers
ContactsMessaging, calling appsTorch, wallpaper, game apps
SMS / Call logsTrue-caller type apps, banking OTP autofillAlmost everything else
Storage / PhotosGallery, editors, WhatsAppBasic utility apps

The test is simple: does the permission match what the app is supposed to do? A maps app without location is useless; a maps app without your contacts is fine. A torch app that wants contacts and location is collecting data, not lighting your way.

How to Review Permissions You've Already Granted

  1. Open Settings → Privacy (or Security & privacy) → Permission manager
  2. Tap a sensitive permission — start with Location, Camera, Microphone
  3. You'll see every app that has it, sorted by what it's allowed to do
  4. Tap any app and change it to "Allow only while in use" or "Don't allow"

Do this for all six sensitive permissions and you'll find at least two or three apps that surprise you. That ten-minute audit is one of the highest-value privacy actions you can take.

Five Rules for Future Popups

  1. Default to "While using the app" — never "Allow all the time". The only apps that genuinely need background location are safety/parental apps, and they'll tell you why.
  2. If the app misbehaves after a denial, decide then — some apps nag or break features without permissions they don't truly need. If a flashlight won't work without contacts, the app doesn't deserve installing.
  3. Denials can be reversed — nothing is permanent. Deny first, and grant it back only when a feature genuinely fails.
  4. Updates can add new permissions — an app you audited months ago may have gained new access. The Permission manager audit is worth repeating every few months.
  5. Be extra strict with sideloaded APKs — apps installed from outside the Play Store get no automated review at all. A modded game asking for SMS access is how banking fraud starts. See our scam safety guide for how these schemes play out.

Special Cases Worth Knowing

  • Accessibility access — the most powerful permission on Android. Legitimate uses exist (screen readers, password managers), but scammers in remote-control scams ask for it too. Never grant it because a phone call told you to.
  • Notification access — lets an app read your OTP messages from notifications. Grant only to apps you deeply trust.
  • "Nearby devices"/Bluetooth — needed for smartwatches and speakers; pointless for most other apps.

Conclusion

Permissions are the quiet trade at the heart of every free app — and many apps ask for far more than their function requires. A ten-minute Permission manager audit, plus the "while using" habit for new popups, puts you back in control. Your data is worth more than the twenty seconds a popup saves you.

Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ)

Will denying a permission break my apps?

Only if the app genuinely needs it — a maps app denied location can't navigate. Modern Android apps are built to handle denials gracefully. The practical approach: deny, use the app normally, and grant back only the specific feature that fails.

Is it safe to allow storage access?

Modern Android splits this into "Photos and videos" (the app sees only images you pick) versus full file access. Prefer the restricted option when offered. Full storage access is reasonable for file managers and galleries — unusual for games and torches.

How do I find out which app used my camera?

On recent Android versions, Settings → Privacy shows indicators and history — a green dot appears in the status bar whenever the camera or microphone is in use. Tapping it shows which app is responsible. Use it whenever you suspect something is watching.
